# 2. Customer profile: customs brokers and importers

Everything here is an **assumption unless marked as a fact**. Validation interviews must
confirm or correct it.

## Segments

| Segment | Description | Why interesting | Main risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| **A. Independent customs brokerage firms (1–20 staff)** | Licensed brokers preparing declarations for many importers | Many invoices from many suppliers; data entry is core work; owner is the buyer | Price sensitivity; may already have tools |
| B. Brokerage desks inside freight forwarders / couriers | High volume, standardised processes | Volume and repeatability | Long sales cycles; corporate IT; existing platforms (e.g. CargoWise) |
| C. Importers preparing their own entries | Distributors, retailers, hardware, auto parts, food importers | Feel the pain directly; may prepare data for their broker | Lower volume each; may not own the declaration step |

**Recommendation:** start with **Segment A, in any country**. It has a single decision-maker, the pain is
direct, and the firms are small enough to start a paid pilot quickly.

## Facts that shape the profile (examples from several markets; the segment exists in every importing country)

- **United States:** about 11,000–14,000+ active licensed customs brokers (CBP / trade.gov).
- **Jamaica:** a licensed customs broker is required where the CIF value of goods
  exceeds US$5,000 (Jamaica Customs Agency), so commercial imports flow through brokers.
- **ASYCUDA World:** used by Belize (all stations since 2012), Jamaica, Trinidad and
  Tobago and many other CARICOM members. ASYCUDA runs in about 102 countries and
  territories worldwide (UNCTAD).
- **Broker fees:** examples include a carrier's Jamaica brokerage package at US$242 per
  bill of lading; a statutory minimum of 200 (local currency) per entry in Saint Lucia;
  and $100–250 per entry commonly cited in the US (vendor source).

## Assumed workflow today

1. Importer emails, WhatsApps or drops off supplier invoices, packing lists and the bill of lading.
2. Clerk reads each invoice and keys header and line items into the customs system (or
   into a spreadsheet first), often converting units, splitting lines by tariff heading,
   and checking totals.
3. Broker classifies goods, determines value and duties, and submits.
4. Customs queries or rejects the entry, leading to an amendment. Errors may cost penalties,
   delays and storage fees.

## Assumed pains (to verify; ranked by expected intensity)

1. Time spent re-keying long invoices (many lines, poor scans, foreign languages).
2. Errors in quantities, values or units causing customs queries, amendments, delays and penalties.
3. Peak-season backlogs; difficulty hiring and training clerks.
4. Missing or inconsistent documents from importers and suppliers.
5. Clients pressing for faster clearance and lower fees.

## Buying behaviour (assumptions)

- **Decision-maker:** the owner or managing broker; clerks influence through usability.
- **Budget logic:** cost per entry versus clerk time and error cost. Brokers price per
  entry, so a per-invoice or per-entry software fee maps to how they think.
- **Triggers:** peak season, losing a clerk, a costly error, a new large client.
- **Objections:**
  - "Our volume is too small."
  - "AI makes mistakes" (answered by the review step and accuracy evidence).
  - "Client data confidentiality" (answered by a privacy policy and DPA).
  - "We already have software."
- **Channels:**
  - broker associations;
  - customs authority broker lists and trade events;
  - referrals between brokers;
  - freight forwarders;
  - LinkedIn;
  - direct visits in a small market.

## Questions only interviews can answer

- How many invoices and lines per month per firm? How long does one take today?
- What do they use now (manual keying, spreadsheets, a broker system, OCR)?
- What does an error cost them, in fees, time and client loss?
- Who pays, how much, and how do they buy software today?
- Is the pain the keying itself, or something else (classification, document chasing,
  customs queries)? If it's something else, the product must change.
